Why the Earn Engine, Not Search

We spent the first few months investigating what seemed like the obvious problem: people sitting on points they can't figure out how to use. A redemption tool. A travel AI. An award search engine. The competitive picture killed that thesis fast.

Seats.aero, PointsYeah, AwardWallet, Gondola, Roame, SeatSpy, and a half-dozen others already serve this market. They all monetize through subscriptions and credit-card affiliate signups, because you can't monetize points directly. Then Odynn raised a $9.5M seed in early 2026 with exactly the product we would have built: search, wallet, redemption, and an AI agent that books awards, all in one platform. They already sell API access that other companies white-label.

The moat around search is also evaporating. Off-the-shelf Claude skills already turn an LLM into a competent travel agent. Open-source flight search libraries on GitHub let anyone build programmatic award search. Reward search is becoming a commodity, and the subscription revenue models funding these tools are getting compressed by AI.

The defensible value is on the earn side. Whoever controls the points currency, the rewards ledger, and the transfer partner network owns the customer relationship. Search tools help you spend points. The earn engine creates them. That is where NonStop Points is focused.

Defining the Target Customer: Segment B (Credit-Averse Middle Income)

1.1 Who They Are

The "New to Rewards" customer is not the unbanked population living paycheck-to-paycheck. They are the debit-preferred, credit-averse middle class, a behavioral cohort, not an economic underclass. These are consumers who could likely qualify for entry-level credit cards but actively choose not to carry them, driven by a generational shift in attitudes toward debt rather than by financial exclusion. Understanding this distinction is critical because it changes the product-market fit argument entirely: these consumers have discretionary spending power, they travel regularly, and they are actively seeking financial tools that align with their values, they simply haven't been offered a rewards product that respects their preference for debit.

1.2 Payment Behavior and Financial Profile

The scale of the debit-preferred market is staggering. Over 90% of US adults have a debit card, with total annual spending exceeding $4 trillion, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta. Meanwhile, approximately 45 million US adults either lack a credit history entirely or have minimal credit activity, effectively shutting them out of earning rewards on everyday purchases and major expenses like travel.

Chime, the largest US neobank, provides the clearest proxy for this demographic. With 25 million total customers and 8.7 million active users as of 2024, Chime's median customer earns $35,000–$70,000, is aged 24–42, and works in service, healthcare, or gig economy roles. The company generated $1.6 billion in revenue in 2024 (up 30.6% YoY) and IPO'd at an $11 billion valuation in 2025, proving that the under-$100k debit-preferred market is not only massive but venture-investable at scale. Chime's highest-value users are "frequent transactors" making 40+ debit card purchases per month, a cohort that grew 15% year-over-year as customers migrate from regional banks.

Travel Behavior: Yes, They Are Travelers

2.1 Travel Frequency and Intent

The most important validation for the NonStop Points thesis is also the simplest: credit-averse middle-income consumers fly, and they fly regularly. The notion that non-credit-card users don't travel is a myth perpetuated by the premium travel industry's marketing, which focuses on affluent cardholders taking international business class trips. The reality of American leisure travel is far more democratized, and budget airlines are the primary vehicle for that democratization.

Middle-income travelers ($50k–$100k) still behave like high-income travelers in terms of trip frequency, they simply spend less per trip by choosing shorter stays, budget accommodations, cheaper destinations, and driving over flying when distance permits. Less than 25% of middle-income travelers reported not traveling for leisure in the past year, compared to 50% of lower-income travelers (under $50k) and just 16% of high-income travelers (over $100k). Among those who do travel, the average US traveler takes 2.8 leisure trips per year, while budget airline customers typically fly 2–4 times annually to vacation destinations.

2.2 Hotel Usage and Accommodation Behavior

Budget airline passengers are not just flying, they're staying. The notion that ULCC customers sleep in their cars or crash with friends is outdated. Allegiant Air's business model explicitly includes "bundle buyers" who purchase hotel and rental-car components through the same transaction, and the company's "Allegiant Vacations" packaging business contributes a mid-to-high-single-digit share of total revenue with above-average margins. In Q4 2025, Allegiant generated $34.9 million from third-party products (primarily vacation packages and travel insurance) and $143 million for the full year, with an average of $8.48 per passenger in third-party product revenue.

Only 28% of middle-income travelers reported not staying in a hotel in the past year, compared to 46% of lower-income travelers and 16% of high-income travelers. Among all travelers surveyed by Barclays, 75% expect to stay at a hotel as their top choice of lodging. The hotel preference is remarkably consistent across income levels, what changes is the quality and price point of the hotel, not the fundamental behavior of staying in one.

2.3 Airlines They Fly and Destination Patterns

These are not business travelers locking in the same Newark-to-Chicago route every week, they are leisure travelers seeking variety, chasing low fares to vacation destinations, and making booking decisions based on price and convenience rather than loyalty to a single carrier. Frontier's network is concentrated in the Western US, Southeastern US, and Caribbean/Mexico, with its Denver hub accounting for roughly 30% of total capacity. Allegiant's network connects secondary and smaller markets to sun destinations like Las Vegas, Orlando, Phoenix, Tampa, and Nashville with flights operating 2–4 times weekly to match weekend and holiday demand.

This leisure-route concentration has two critical implications for a transferable points product. First, these travelers are not locked into a single airline's route network, they fly Frontier to Denver, Allegiant to Vegas, Southwest to visit family. A single-program debit card is of limited value to a customer whose next trip might be on a completely different airline. Second, the high ancillary spend per passenger on budget airlines ($60–$90 per passenger on bag fees, seat selection, priority boarding, and in-flight purchases) represents a massive unmonetized rewards opportunity. Every one of these transactions currently earns zero points for the debit-preferred customer.

2.4 Travel as Non-Negotiable Spending

The Caravelo State of Travel Subscriptions 2026 report, drawing on over 600,000 subscription-powered flights across 200,000+ travelers, confirms a behavioral shift that matters here: younger travelers don't treat flights as a luxury. They treat them as a recurring budget line.

Roughly half of Gen Z and millennial travelers consider travel spending non-negotiable, ranking it alongside rent and groceries. Three out of four say they will travel significantly more than older generations. This reframes the demand question entirely. The target customer isn't someone who might fly once they have rewards. They're already flying. They're already spending. They're earning nothing for it.

The booking experience itself shows how broken the status quo is. US travelers now visit 277 web pages before booking a trip (flight, hotel, and extras combined), up from just 38 in 2013 (Caravelo 2026, citing Expedia Group). 72% of travelers abandon the booking process because they feel overwhelmed. A single rewards ecosystem with simplified booking is exactly what this generation is asking for.

The Rewards Gap: What They're Missing and Why It Matters

3.1 The "Loyalty Without Earning" Paradox

The most compelling evidence for the NonStop Points opportunity is the vast gap between loyalty program enrollment and credit card penetration. Allegiant Air illustrates this paradox perfectly: the airline has 18 million active Allways Rewards members but only 545,000 co-brand credit card holders, a 33-to-1 ratio. This means that 97% of Allegiant's loyalty members have no credit card mechanism to earn points on everyday spending. They earn points only through flight purchases, which for a leisure traveler taking 2–3 trips per year, accumulates at a glacial pace.

Source: Allegiant Air Q4 2025 earnings; Allways Rewards program data

This pattern repeats across the industry. Southwest Airlines has millions of Rapid Rewards members who fly regularly but don't have (or don't want) a Southwest credit card. United's MileagePlus program has over 100 million members globally, but only a fraction carry a United co-brand credit card. The 2025 debit card launches from all three brands are an explicit acknowledgment of this gap, an attempt to give their loyalty members a way to earn points through their preferred payment method. But each solution is siloed: Southwest's debit card earns only Rapid Rewards points, United's earns only MileagePlus miles, and Wyndham's earns only Wyndham Rewards points.

The Loyalty Crisis: Why the Incumbents Are Vulnerable

The $120 billion airline loyalty empire is a financial instrument, not a customer relationship.

The numbers look spectacular on the surface. The world's top 10 airline loyalty programs are collectively valued at over $120 billion, more than four times the airline industry's global profits in 2024. Delta's loyalty arm turned what would have been a -2.5% operating margin into 10.5%. Wall Street values the loyalty divisions of the Big Three US carriers higher than the airlines themselves.

But the commercial success is masking a structural failure. These programs are built around retention mechanics (thresholds, sunk costs, status tiers, forfeiture penalties), not trust. Customers stay because leaving feels expensive, not because they feel valued.

The data from Boston Consulting Group confirms it: only 13% of frequent flyer members fly exclusively with their loyalty airline. The other 87% regularly choose competitors despite membership status. People are more loyal to a $15 Netflix subscription than to a $500 plane ticket. An iSeatz study found that 92% of loyalty providers believe their programs deliver real value. Ask the actual customers, and only 50% agree.

The Two-Customer Thesis

NonStop Points serves two distinct customer segments with one product.

The first is the mass market: 120 million debit-preferred adults spending $5 trillion a year and earning nothing for it. This is the earn engine. Every swipe generates interchange revenue and deposits points into a rewards ecosystem that didn't exist for them before. The product is simple: a debit card that earns transferable travel points. No credit check, no annual fee trap, no rewards they can't figure out how to use.

The second customer is the savvy traveler. This is the person who already pays $30 to $80 a month across seats.aero subscriptions, AwardWallet, Daily Drop, and similar tools. Many of them carry a Charles Schwab debit card for its zero foreign transaction fees. Thrifty Traveler called it "the best debit card for international travel," and it has built a cult following among points enthusiasts despite offering zero rewards.

The premium NonStop tier folds all of those tools into one subscription: award search, booking, a travel AI concierge, and transferable points on every purchase. Instead of paying for four separate subscriptions and earning nothing, the savvy traveler gets the same functionality while also earning rewards.

Caravelo's 2026 data supports this model. Over two-thirds of flight subscription travelers are under 45, exactly our target demo. And when asked why they subscribed, the top motivators were predictability and freedom, not price savings. Nearly half of all surveyed travelers said convenience was the number one reason they'd commit to a "Netflix-like" travel subscription (Travelport, cited in Caravelo 2026).

The second group gives the first group confidence. If power users who understand points deeply choose to carry the NonStop card, that signals credibility to first-time rewards earners. And the mass market's transaction volume funds the interchange economics that make the whole thing work.

The Multi-Program Advantage: Why Transferability Wins

9.1 The Single-Program Trap

The fundamental limitation of every debit rewards card launched in 2025 is single-program lock-in. Southwest's debit card earns only Rapid Rewards points. United's earns only MileagePlus miles. Wyndham's earns only Wyndham Rewards points. For a customer who travels across multiple brands, a Southwest flight to Vegas, a Wyndham stay, a United trip to visit family, this means carrying three separate debit cards and managing three separate points balances, each with its own expiration policy, redemption rules, and transfer restrictions. The fragmentation is user-hostile and economically inefficient.

This is precisely the problem that transferable points solved in the premium credit card market. Chase Ultimate Rewards, American Express Membership Rewards, Capital One Miles, and Citi ThankYou Points all allow cardholders to earn a single currency and then transfer it to dozens of airline and hotel partners at 1:1 ratios. The flexibility to "shop around for the best award availability," "take advantage of transfer bonuses," and "avoid award chart devaluations from any one program" makes transferable points objectively more valuable than single-program miles.

💡 The NonStop Value Proposition: A customer earning "Universal Points" on everyday debit spending could transfer those points to Southwest for a domestic flight, to Wyndham for a hotel stay, to United for an international trip, or to any other partner in the network, all from a single debit card and a single points balance. This is not a marginal improvement over the 2025 debit card launches; it is a category-defining differentiation.

9.2 The Bilt Parallel: Transferable Points for Non-Traditional Spend

The closest existing comparable to NonStop Points' proposed model is Bilt Rewards, which has proven that consumers will engage intensely with a transferable points program tied to non-traditional spending categories. Bilt allows members to earn transferable points on rent payments, the largest monthly expense for most renters, and transfer them to 15+ airline and hotel partners including American Airlines, Southwest, United, Hyatt, and IHG. Bilt's transferable points model has been so successful that the company recently launched Bilt Card 2.0 with three tiers, expanded earning categories, and additional benefits, all built around the core value proposition of earning flexible points on everyday spending.

The Bilt parallel is instructive for NonStop Points in three ways. First, it proves that consumers highly value transferable points over single-program currency. Second, it demonstrates that non-traditional spending categories can drive significant points accumulation. Third, Bilt's success attracted major partnership interest from real estate companies, landlords, and financial institutions, suggesting that NonStop Points could similarly attract B2B partnerships with airlines, hotel chains, and travel platforms.

The key difference: Bilt is a credit card. It requires a credit check, a credit line, and the willingness to carry a credit product, precisely the barriers that exclude NonStop Points' target customer. NonStop Points would be the first transferable points program built natively on debit, combining Bilt's multi-program flexibility with the debt-free, credit-check-free accessibility that debit-preferred consumers demand.

The generational payment shift is the strongest macro tailwind for NonStop Points. One Gen X respondent summarized the mindset: "I like the idea of a debit card because it means you're using money that you've already earned and not betting on a future that may or may not come financially." This is a values-based payment philosophy that prioritizes financial control and transparency over the rewards optimization that credit cards offer.

Gen Z and millennials are not loyalty program skeptics, they are loyalty program enthusiasts who haven't found programs designed for their payment preferences. NonStop Points' proposed model, simple earning on everyday debit spend, transparent transfer ratios, instant redemption through partner programs, aligns precisely with these preferences. The product is not trying to convince Gen Z to want travel rewards; it is giving them a mechanism to earn the rewards they already want through the payment method they already prefer.

Addressing the VC Skepticism: Direct Rebuttals

10.1 "These People Aren't Travelers"

❌ The Myth: Debit-preferred, middle-income consumers don't travel enough to care about travel rewards.

✅ The Data: Frontier Airlines carried 33 million passengers in 2025, Allegiant has 18 million active loyalty members, and Southwest carried 140+ million customers, the vast majority leisure travelers in the target demographic. The Barclays Travel Report found that 82% of all travelers would have to travel differently or not at all without loyalty rewards. Middle-income travelers take 2.8 leisure trips per year on average, the same frequency as higher-income travelers, just at lower per-trip spend.

10.2 "They Only Want Cash Back"

❌ The Myth: 82% of consumers prefer cash back over travel rewards, so this market won't engage.

✅ The Data: The "cash back preference" statistic is a misleading aggregate. Among higher-income consumers with transferable points cards, travel redemption rates are dramatically higher because the value proposition is obvious once experienced. 88% of millennials say they couldn't take their desired trips without rewards, this is not a population that prefers cash back; it is a population that has never had a travel rewards product accessible through their preferred payment method. Gen Z travelers with rewards cards redeem for travel at a 73% rate, the highest of any generation.

10.3 "The Market Is Too Small"

❌ The Myth: There aren't enough of these people to build a venture-scale business.

✅ The Data: The addressable population is 22+ million active leisure travelers in the core demographic, with a broader population of 49 million credit-averse adults and a total debit spending base of $4+ trillion annually. Chime's $11 billion IPO valuation and $1.6 billion annual revenue from 8.7 million active users proves that the under-$100k debit-preferred market is venture-investable at scale. The 2025 debit card launches from Wyndham, Southwest, and United represent multi-million-dollar product investments that wouldn't have been made if the market were insufficiently large.

10.4 "Debit Rewards Don't Work Economically"

❌ The Myth: Debit interchange (1–1.5%) is too low to fund meaningful rewards vs. credit interchange (2–3%).

✅ The Data: The economics are viable through a blended model: interchange revenue + monthly subscription fees ($4–7/mo) + points breakage (unredeemed points) + B2B partnership revenue (airlines/hotels paying for customer access). The 2025 debit card launches from three major travel brands all use the same Galileo/Sunrise Banks infrastructure and are structured as profitable standalone products, not loss leaders. NonStop Points' transferable points model actually improves economics by enabling B2B partnerships at scale.

10.5 "Won't Customers Just Game the System?"

The Myth: Rewards customers will sign up, earn points, and churn, or use the card only for category bonuses without building real engagement.
The Data: Caravelo's 2026 report analyzed over 600,000 subscription-powered flights across six airline programs. Only 13% to 28% of subscription travelers replaced flights they would have taken anyway (true cannibalization). The majority was either incremental demand (trips that wouldn't have happened without the subscription) or competitive wins (flights taken from a rival airline). Well-designed rewards products don't just redistribute existing spend. They create new behavior.

Strategic Positioning: Why "New to Rewards" Is the Right First Segment

11.1 Lowest Cost to Acquire

The "New to Rewards" customer (Segment 1) represents the lowest customer acquisition cost (CAC) for NonStop Points because they have no existing loyalty program relationships to displace. Unlike premium credit card users who are already enrolled in Chase Ultimate Rewards, Amex Membership Rewards, or airline-specific programs, the debit-preferred traveler is a loyalty "blank slate." They are not being asked to switch from a competing product; they are being offered a product where none previously existed. This is the same acquisition dynamic that powered Chime's growth: neobank customers weren't switching from Chase, they were opening their first digital bank account.

The marketing channels for this segment are also highly efficient. Budget airlines' own customer bases, 18 million Allways Rewards members at Allegiant, millions of Rapid Rewards members at Southwest, 33 million Frontier passengers, represent a captive audience of pre-qualified prospects who are already enrolled in a single-program loyalty system and would benefit from a multi-program alternative. These airlines have a direct relationship with the customer through email, mobile apps, and the booking experience, providing low-cost distribution channels.

11.2 Highest Willingness to Pay

The "New to Rewards" customer has the highest willingness to pay a monthly fee for a debit rewards product because the perceived value is highest relative to their current state. A customer who has never earned a travel point in their life will perceive significant value in earning their first 10,000 points, enough for a free domestic flight or hotel night. The existing 2025 debit cards have established pricing benchmarks: $4–$7/month, waived with a $2,000–$2,500 average balance. For a customer spending $500–$1,000/month on a debit card, earning 1x transferable points on that spend (6,000–12,000 points annually) represents $60–$120+ in travel value, a 7–15x return on a $48–$84 annual fee.

11.3 Proving the Model for Segments 2 and 3

The strategic sequencing of customer segments, New to Rewards first, then Premium Customers, then B2B, is validated by the competitive environment and infrastructure development timeline. Segment 1 proves that debit-based transferable points are viable, builds the user base and transaction volume needed to negotiate favorable B2B partnerships, and generates the data required to demonstrate engagement metrics to premium customers (Segment 2) and business partners (Segment 3). The 2025 single-program debit card launches have already validated the core debit rewards thesis; NonStop Points' multi-program innovation is the logical next step that the market is structurally moving toward.

Key Risks and Mitigations

🔒 Regulatory Risk

Durbin Amendment caps debit interchange for banks over $10B. Mitigation: Partner with a smaller issuing bank (like Sunrise Banks, a CDFI under $10B) to preserve higher interchange, the same model all 2025 debit cards use.

💰 Unit Economics Risk

Debit interchange (1–1.5%) is lower than credit (2–3%). Mitigation: Blend interchange + monthly subscriptions ($4–7/mo) + points breakage + B2B partnership revenue. Three major brands already prove this model works.

🏢 Competition from Incumbents

Chase or Amex could launch their own debit product. Mitigation: They have a structural conflict, debit would cannibalize their credit profits. All 2025 launches came from airlines/hotels, not issuers.

📢 Customer Acquisition Risk

Need distribution at scale. Mitigation: 100M+ budget airline loyalty members are pre-qualified prospects. All 2025 cards acquired through existing loyalty channels without paid advertising.
